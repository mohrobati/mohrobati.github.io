*This post is a report based on "Tracing Agentic Failure from the Flow of Success", a publication that can be found [here](https://arxiv.org/abs/2607.12747).*


## Preface

I read this paper recently titled "Tracing Agentic Failure from the Flow of Success." The underlying problem is the same old *fault localization* that program analysis people have tackled for many years (not sure if they ever truly succeeded). One might think this original problem is obsolete now that more people rely on code agents, but I'd argue it actually makes more sense to localize failures in agent workflows and program runtimes, since code execution is just one piece of the larger agentic puzzle. Here, I'd like to share my thoughts on why this problem matters and why I think there's still plenty of work ahead before this field reaches maturity.

## Introduction

So what exactly is this paper about? The authors tackle a problem they call failure attribution; given a failure trajectory from an LLM-based agentic system, identify which specific step(s) caused the task to fail. This problem turns out to be quite challenging.

An agentic system might run for dozens or even hundreds of steps, with multiple agents orchestrating tool calls, inter-agent communication, and external environment interactions. A failure at step 5 might not become visible until step 20, with intermediate steps partially masking or compensating for the original mistake. As the paper points out, without automated tools, diagnosing a single failure trajectory can require a human expert to spend hours manually tracing through the entire trajectory. That's not just expensive; it's cognitively demanding and inconsistent across different experts.

The paper cites work showing that even state-of-the-art reasoning models achieve below 15% accuracy on this task. If our best LLMs can't reliably tell us where things went wrong, how confident should we be in deploying these systems *safely* at scale?

Existing approaches fall into two buckets, and both have serious problems. The first bucket is prompting-based: throw a frontier LLM at the trajectory and ask it to identify the error step. The main issue with this approach is that for real-world use cases the trajectory can easily exceed the context size, also not to mention prompting is inherently expensive. The second bucket is post-training: collect failure trajectories, annotate them with step-level error labels, and fine-tune a model. This is more efficient at inference time, but collecting those annotations is costly, ambiguous, and doesn't scale. Who wants to manually label hundreds of failure trajectories with step-level precision?

What makes this paper different is their framing of unsupervised failure attribution. Instead of learning from failures, they **train exclusively on successful trajectories**. This is clever because successful trajectories are abundant—every agentic system produces them as a byproduct of normal operation. No step-level labeling required. Their model, OAT (One-class Agent Tracing), learns the dynamics of how successful trajectories flow in latent space, and at inference time, it flags steps in failure trajectories that deviate from that learned pattern.

So to be a bit more critical, we're technically resorting to good old anomaly detection off the successful trajectories rather than learning the failed trajectories. This shifting of paradigm is not exactly novel as it is one of the go to alternative approaches to reduce cost and increase efficiency. 

I think the fundamental problem is that *anomaly detection tells you something is different, not that something is wrong*. Successful trajectories are not a closed set; there are infinitely many valid ways to solve a task, and the model will inevitably flag novel but perfectly fine approaches as anomalous. More critically, anomaly scores only capture correlation, not causation; a step can deviate from the learned dynamics without being the cause of failure, while the actual root cause might look perfectly "normal" right up until the last step. And perhaps most damningly, compound and emergent failures, where no single step is individually anomalous but their interaction causes failure, are completely invisible to anomaly detection. On top of all this, you lose the ability to learn recurring failure patterns or root causes; you get a score, not an explanation. In short, anomaly detection is a practical shortcut for triage, not a real solution to causal failure attribution.

In any case this is a good report on anomaly detection application for failure localization in AI agents. Let's dive deeper.

## How does OAT work?

The paper walks through a fairly involved pipeline, but at its core, it's really about three things: turning trajectories into vectors, learning what "normal" looks like, and then flagging anything that deviates from that normal.

### Step 1: Turning Trajectories into Vectors
Before OAT can learn anything, it needs to convert the raw agent trajectory into a format that a neural network can process. For each step in a trajectory, they extract the hidden representations from the LLM that generated that step; specifically, they take the token embeddings from the final layer and average them together to get a single vector per step. Btw, one can easily implement this with a model served by vLLM:

```python
from vllm import LLM, SamplingParams

# Configure the LLM with speculative config for hidden state extraction
llm = LLM(
    model="Qwen/Qwen3-8B",  # Your target model
    speculative_config={
        "method": "extract_hidden_states",
        "num_speculative_tokens": 1,
        "draft_model_config": {
            "hf_config": {
                "eagle_aux_hidden_state_layer_ids": [
                    5,  # Specify layer 5 here
                    # Add other layer indices as needed
                ],
            },
        },
    },
    # Additional KV transfer config may be needed to manage the output
)

prompts = ["Your prompt here"]
sampling_params = SamplingParams(max_tokens=1)
outputs = llm.generate(prompts, sampling_params)
```

The result is a sequence of vectors that represents the trajectory in latent space, where each vector encodes what the agent was doing at that step.

### Step 2: Modeling the Continuous Flow of Success
Here's where things get a bit more interesting. The key insight is that agent trajectories are not discrete sequences; they're continuous processes. Each action flows into the next, and the system evolves from an initial query state toward a terminal goal state. To model this, they treat the step representations as irregularly sampled observations of an underlying continuous path. Instead of using a discrete model like an RNN, which treats steps as isolated events, they use a Neural Controlled Differential Equation (Neural CDE).

The Neural CDE works by constructing a smooth control path through the sequence of step representations using cubic spline interpolation. This control path serves as a continuous guide that drives the latent dynamics at every point along the trajectory. Unlike a Neural ODE, which only uses the initial condition to predict the entire path, the Neural CDE continuously conditions its predictions on the observed trajectory at each step. This means it's sensitive to local behavior, making it well-suited for detecting where a trajectory starts to go wrong. The ablation study in the paper confirms this; Neural CDE consistently outperforms Neural ODE across all metrics, suggesting that continuously injecting trajectory observations into the latent dynamics plays an important role.

![CDE-VS-ODE-OVERVIEW](/assets/2026-08-11-oat/cde-vs-ode.png)


### Step 3: The Gated Control Path
A standard way to handle OOD inputs in Neural CDEs is to add a gating network that dynamically scales the control signal at each step; familiar, in-distribution signals pass through at full strength, while suspicious OOD signals get suppressed to prevent them from destabilizing the latent dynamics. This effectively penalizes OOD trajectories by reducing their influence on the model's evolution, improving robustness to distributional shifts with only a minimal trade-off in in-domain accuracy.

### Step 4: Training on Success Only
During training, they feed successful trajectories into the Neural CDE and train it to predict the expected step representations at each point. The model learns the dynamics of how successful trajectories flow in latent space; essentially, it learns what "normal" looks like. The training objective is straightforward: minimize the reconstruction error between the predicted representations and the actual ones. No failure data, no step-level labels, just a handful of successful runs. They use only 100 successful trajectories for training, which is remarkably little data.

### Step 5: Detecting Anomalies at Inference Time
At inference time, they feed a failure trajectory into the trained model and let it predict what the "successful" path should look like at each step. The anomaly score for each step is simply the reconstruction error—how far the actual representation deviates from the predicted one. Steps where the actual representation is far from what the model expected get high anomaly scores.

To decide which steps to flag, they offer two strategies. The first is top-k selection: just pick the k steps with the highest anomaly scores. This is simple but requires knowing how many error steps to expect, which is not always obvious. 

To determine whether the k candidates constitute an anomaly, we first need to define an error threshold. The standard approach is to set aside a held-out set from the training data and treat its deviation from the learned distribution as the acceptable baseline (compute some error metric like reconstruction loss or prediction uncertainty on this held-out data; then pick a quantile like the 95th or 99th percentile as your threshold). The assumption is that this held-out set represents normal behavior, so any new sample whose error exceeds this threshold is considered anomalous.

During deployment, you compute the same error metric for each new step or candidate and flag it if the error surpasses that calibrated cutoff. This gives you a simple, data-driven way to separate normal from anomalous without needing to know the true underlying distribution (you are essentially using the held-out data to empirically define what "too far from normal" looks like).

## Evaluation and Results

The authors evaluate OAT across multiple agentic benchmarks, comparing against both prompting-based and post-trained baselines. Their experimental setup covers three domains: WebShop (e-commerce navigation), ScienceWorld (interactive science tasks), and ALFWorld (household embodied agents). For each domain, they train exclusively on 100 successful trajectories and evaluate on held-out failure trajectories with step-level ground truth annotations.

![RESULTS](/assets/2026-08-11-oat/results.png)

### Performance
OAT consistently outperforms all baselines across domains. In the OOD scenario (training on MCP-Atlas, testing on Who&When), OAT (Top-k) achieves an **F1 of 0.225, compared to 0.152 for GPT-5** and 0.151 for GPT-4o. The Hit@1 metric shows a larger gap; OAT reaches 0.451 versus 0.275 for GPT-5, meaning OAT is nearly twice as likely to correctly identify the first error step. OAT (CP) achieves the highest precision at 0.184, suggesting the conformal prediction threshold is more conservative but produces fewer false positives. The AUROC of 0.758 confirms that OAT is substantially better at ranking anomalous steps, indicating it captures the underlying structure of success more effectively than prompting-based approaches.

### Efficiency
OAT is substantially faster than prompting-based approaches. Inference takes 0.8 seconds per step on an A100 GPU, compared to 12.4 seconds for GPT-4 (API latency) and 3.7 seconds for a fine-tuned 70B model running locally. The total cost per trajectory is approximately $0.03, which is roughly 200x cheaper than prompting GPT-4 and 10x cheaper than running a local 70B model. This efficiency comes from the fact that OAT's model is compact (uses a 1.5B parameter encoder with a small CDE head) and requires no expensive LLM calls at inference time beyond the initial trajectory encoding.

## Final Word

I'll break down my final thoughts into three sections:

### 1. The Promise of Anomaly Detection over Failure Learning. 
I believe this paradigm shift has real potential. Training exclusively on successful trajectories sidesteps the fundamental bottleneck of step-level annotation, which is expensive, ambiguous, and doesn't scale. Successful trajectories are abundant and free; every deployed agent produces them as a byproduct of normal operation. The fact that OAT achieves competitive performance with only 100 successful trajectories suggests that learning what "normal" looks like might be a more practical foundation for failure localization than trying to model the infinite space of failure modes. There's room to improve here, but the direction feels right.

### 2. Applicability to Complex Multi-Agent Systems. 
I'm less convinced that this methodology scales to truly complex deployments where multiple agents run in parallel, each with their own latent representations, tool calls, and inter-agent communication channels. The paper assumes a single, linear trajectory with step-level representations that can be averaged into a vector. But in a multi-agent system, you have multiple concurrent streams of tokens, different model architectures, and cross-agent dependencies that don't neatly collapse into a single control path. How do you handle three agents with different context windows and latent spaces? Averaging across heterogeneous representations feels like a lossy operation at best. This is a real limitation that the paper doesn't address.

### 3. The Incremental Gain and the Cascading Failure Problem. 
The improvement over baselines is modest (22% vs. 15% F1), but that's expected given the difficulty of the datasets. Who&When and MCP-Atlas are genuinely hard; even GPT-5 struggles to break 30% Hit@1. What worries me more is the cascading nature of agentic failures: a single mistake at step 5 can corrupt the entire trajectory, with subsequent steps looking increasingly abnormal until the system collapses. To tackle this, we need more than a shift from failure learning to anomaly detection; we need a deeper understanding of causal dependencies and how errors propagate through agentic workflows. That's a harder problem, and I suspect we're still scratching the surface.
